**The Effective Facilitator**

**Full Product and Curriculum Specification**

**Nine Disciplines for Human Judgment in the Age of AI**

**1. Programme identity**

**Positioning statement**

**The Effective Facilitator is a developmental learning programme for
people working and making decisions with AI. Through nine personal
disciplines, it strengthens the judgment, self-awareness and human
agency required to navigate authority, ambiguity and control.**

**Core premise**

**AI makes generation abundant. Human judgment becomes the constraint.**

Artificial intelligence can generate content, options, analyses,
recommendations and proposed decisions at extraordinary speed. What
remains distinctly human is the responsibility to determine:

-   what to trust;

-   what to question;

-   when to act;

-   when to override a system;

-   when people must participate;

-   what values should govern the process;

-   and when ownership matters more than apparent optimisation.

**Programme principle**

**Facilitation begins with the facilitation of self.**

The learner is not being trained only to facilitate meetings. The
learner is developing the capacity to manage their own relationship
with:

-   authority;

-   ambiguity;

-   control;

-   attention;

-   responsibility;

-   judgment;

-   creativity;

-   and action.

**2. Source and attribution**

The programme is inspired by and adapted from:

**Jon C. Jenkins and Maureen R. Jenkins, *The 9 Disciplines of a
Facilitator: Leading Groups by Transforming Yourself*.**

The source framework identifies nine personal disciplines:

-   Detachment

-   Engagement

-   Focus

-   Awareness

-   Action

-   Presence

-   Interior Dialogue

-   Intentionality

-   Sense of Wonder

It presents a discipline as a developmental path for acquiring skills or
competencies through study and practice, rather than as a fixed
personality characteristic.

The source also organises the disciplines into three developmental
paths:

-   **Regarding Others:** Detachment, Focus and Engagement

-   **Regarding Myself:** Interior Dialogue, Sense of Wonder and
    Intentionality

-   **Regarding Life:** Awareness, Presence and Action

**Recommended attribution statement**

This statement should appear on the About page, programme introduction
and final certificate information:

**The Effective Facilitator is inspired by and adapted from *The 9
Disciplines of a Facilitator: Leading Groups by Transforming Yourself*
by Jon C. Jenkins and Maureen R. Jenkins. The original framework was
developed for facilitators leading groups. This programme extends the
disciplines into the context of self-facilitation, human judgment and
responsible decision-making in the age of artificial intelligence.**

**Intellectual distinction**

The following elements come directly from the source framework:

-   the nine disciplines;

-   the three developmental paths;

-   the idea of discipline as learned developmental practice;

-   the central tensions between disciplines;

-   the emphasis on transformation of the facilitator;

-   and many of the foundational descriptions of each discipline.

The following are programme adaptations:

-   application to AI-supported work and decision-making;

-   the authority, ambiguity and control lens;

-   app-based assessments;

-   scoring logic;

-   situational judgment exercises;

-   human-agency measures;

-   organisational AI scenarios;

-   progress tracking;

-   and the personal operating practice.

**3. Product purpose**

The app should help users answer three questions:

**Authority**

Who or what am I allowing to shape this decision?

**Ambiguity**

How do I respond when the answer remains incomplete?

**Control**

When should I direct, intervene, release or allow emergence?

The programme does not aim to make users suspicious of AI. It aims to
help them use AI deliberately without confusing:

-   assistance with authority;

-   fluency with truth;

-   efficiency with effectiveness;

-   prediction with destiny;

-   generation with imagination;

-   or automation with responsibility.

**Primary users**

-   Leaders and managers

-   Public-sector professionals

-   Organisational transformation teams

-   Educators and trainers

-   Consultants and facilitators

-   Policy and programme officers

-   Professionals using AI for analysis or decision support

**Secondary users**

-   Students preparing for AI-mediated workplaces

-   Creative professionals

-   Entrepreneurs

-   Human-resource teams

-   Learning and development departments

-   Organisations implementing digital transformation

**User needs**

The likely user does not need another general introduction to AI. They
need help with questions such as:

-   Am I relying too heavily on AI recommendations?

-   Have I thought independently before prompting?

-   Am I automating a bad process?

-   Have I preserved human judgment and appeal?

-   Am I using AI to avoid a difficult responsibility?

-   Has a polished output narrowed my imagination?

-   Should this decision be optimised, or should people own it?

-   What remains my responsibility after delegation?

**5. Programme outcomes**

By the end of the programme, the learner should be able to:

1.  Explain the nine disciplines and their relationship to AI-supported
    work.

2.  Identify personal tendencies involving authority, ambiguity and
    control.

3.  Distinguish AI assistance from legitimate decision authority.

4.  Evaluate generated outputs without being unduly influenced by polish
    or confidence.

5.  Preserve independent thought before consulting AI.

6.  recognise when AI is narrowing rather than expanding imagination.

7.  Identify when human participation and ownership matter.

8.  Take responsibility for AI-assisted decisions and communications.

9.  Act responsibly when certainty is unavailable.

10. recognise assumptions, biases and missing stakeholders.

11. decide what should be automated, redesigned or retained as human.

12. create a personal practice for responsible AI use.

**11. Standard module template**

Every full module should use the following structure.

**Section 1 --- Opening dilemma**

A realistic scenario is presented before teaching begins.

The learner selects or writes an initial response.

Purpose:

-   expose existing judgment;

-   reduce answer rehearsal;

-   provide a comparison point after learning.

**Section 2 --- Why this discipline matters**

A concise explanation connects:

-   the original facilitation issue;

-   self-facilitation;

-   AI-supported work;

-   and the learner's everyday decisions.

**Section 3 --- Definition**

Includes:

-   source-informed definition;

-   AI-era definition;

-   what the discipline is;

-   what it is not.

**Section 4 --- The central tension**

Each discipline should be understood as a balance.

Example:

Detachment must be balanced with Engagement. Without Detachment, the
learner may control. Without Engagement, the learner may withdraw.

**Section 5 --- Behavioural continuum**

Three states:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Underused**              **Balanced**           **Overused**
  -------------------------- ---------------------- ----------------------
  Behavioural description    Effective expression   Distorted expression

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Section 6 --- AI-era failure modes**

Shows how the discipline may fail in AI use.

**Section 7 --- Recognise it**

Includes:

-   short scenarios;

-   rank-order questions;

-   observation-versus-interpretation tasks;

-   or identify-the-assumption activities.

**Section 8 --- Self-assessment**

Five to eight behavioural items.

**Section 9 --- Practice**

A real-world activity.

**Section 10 --- Evidence reflection**

Required fields:

-   What was the situation?

-   What did you notice?

-   What did you do?

-   What happened?

-   What evidence supports your conclusion?

-   What would you repeat?

-   What would you change?

**Section 11 --- Revisit the opening dilemma**

The learner answers the opening scenario again.

The app compares the two answers.

**Section 12 --- Commitment**

The learner completes:

The next time I encounter this situation, I will...

**12. Baseline assessment**

**Assessment purpose**

The assessment should identify developmental tendencies. It should not
claim to be a validated psychological instrument unless it is later
professionally validated.

It measures:

-   self-reported behaviour;

-   situational judgment;

-   independent thought;

-   AI-use patterns;

-   confidence;

-   and reflection.

**Assessment structure**

**Part A --- Behavioural statements**

Five statements per discipline for the MVP.

Total:

45 items

Each is rated:

1.  Almost never

2.  Rarely

3.  Sometimes

4.  Often

5.  Almost always

At least one item per discipline should be reverse-scored.

**Part B --- Situational judgment**

One scenario per discipline.

Total:

9 scenarios

The learner chooses the strongest response from four options.

**Part C --- Confidence calibration**

After each scenario:

How confident are you in your answer?

Scale:

-   Not confident

-   Slightly confident

-   Moderately confident

-   Very confident

-   Completely confident

This allows the app to identify:

-   high judgment and calibrated confidence;

-   sound judgment but low confidence;

-   weak judgment with high confidence;

-   and uncertainty requiring further learning.

**Part D --- AI-use reflection**

Five non-scored questions:

-   What types of decisions do you use AI to support?

-   Have you ever accepted an AI answer without checking it?

-   Do you usually think before prompting?

-   Have you used AI to avoid a difficult task or conversation?

-   What responsibility do you believe remains human?

**13. Baseline assessment items**

**Detachment**

1.  I can discard an AI-generated answer even after investing
    significant time refining it.

2.  When a group chooses an option I would not choose, I can continue
    supporting the process constructively.

3.  I distinguish the quality of an idea from whether I personally
    created or prompted it.

4.  I can acknowledge that a persuasive output may still be wrong.

5.  I find it difficult to release an AI-assisted solution once I have
    presented it to others. **Reverse-scored**

**Focus**

1.  I define the decision I need to make before asking AI for options.

2.  I establish criteria before comparing generated recommendations.

3.  I know when an output is sufficient to move forward.

4.  I can distinguish relevant information from impressive but
    unnecessary detail.

5.  I continue generating alternatives even after the useful decision
    point has been reached. **Reverse-scored**

**Engagement**

1.  I accept responsibility for the consequences of AI-assisted work.

2.  I consider how a decision will affect people who were not involved
    in producing it.

3.  I intervene when automation is reducing meaningful participation.

4.  I verify important AI-generated information before acting on it.

5.  When an AI-supported decision causes harm, responsibility primarily
    belongs to the system. **Reverse-scored**

**Interior Dialogue**

1.  I form an initial view before asking AI for a recommendation.

2.  I can identify which personal motives are influencing my response.

3.  I compare AI advice with my own experience and values.

4.  I question whether my intuition reflects insight, fear or bias.

5.  A confident AI answer usually becomes my starting position.
    **Reverse-scored**

**Sense of Wonder**

1.  I can stay with an important question without demanding an immediate
    answer.

2.  I develop my own possibilities before reviewing generated examples.

3.  I notice when a polished answer has narrowed my imagination.

4.  I remain open to outcomes not predicted by historical patterns.

5.  An idea has little value unless I can immediately explain how it
    will work. **Reverse-scored**

**Intentionality**

1.  I can clearly explain why I am using AI for a particular task.

2.  I identify what must remain human before choosing what to automate.

3.  I examine whether convenience is replacing responsibility.

4.  I align AI use with the purpose and values of the work.

5.  I usually select the tool before clarifying the outcome I need.
    **Reverse-scored**

**Awareness**

1.  I distinguish facts, assumptions and AI-generated claims.

2.  I consider whose experience may be missing from the data.

3.  I recognise how my preferences influence what I ask AI to produce.

4.  I investigate the limitations of an AI tool before relying on it.

5.  A complete-looking answer reduces my need to seek other
    perspectives. **Reverse-scored**

**Presence**

1.  I notice human reactions that may not appear in a transcript or
    dashboard.

2.  I can remain attentive without immediately reaching for a
    technological solution.

3.  I listen fully before generating a response.

4.  I adjust a plan when the real situation differs from the system's
    prediction.

5.  I give more weight to the recorded data than to what people are
    experiencing in the moment. **Reverse-scored**

**Action**

1.  I can act responsibly without waiting for complete certainty.

2.  I identify the smallest responsible action that can test an
    assumption.

3.  I weigh the risk of delay as well as the risk of acting.

4.  I review the consequences of an AI-assisted decision and adjust
    accordingly.

5.  I often ask for more analysis when the real difficulty is committing
    to a decision. **Reverse-scored**

**14. Scoring logic**

**Behavioural score**

Each item is scored from 1 to 5.

Reverse-scored items are converted:

-   1 becomes 5

-   2 becomes 4

-   3 remains 3

-   4 becomes 2

-   5 becomes 1

For each discipline:

Behavioural score = total item score ÷ maximum available score × 100

Example:

A learner scores 19 out of 25:

19 ÷ 25 × 100 = 76

**Situational judgment score**

Each scenario option receives a score:

-   Strongest response: 4

-   Partially effective: 3

-   Limited response: 2

-   Weakest response: 1

Converted to percentage:

Scenario score = option score ÷ 4 × 100

**Confidence calibration**

Confidence does not increase the capability score.

It creates a separate indicator.

Example:

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Judgment**   **Confidence**   **Interpretation**
  -------------- ---------------- -----------------------------------------
  High           High             Assured application

  High           Low              Sound judgment, low confidence

  Low            High             Possible blind spot

  Low            Low              Recognised development need
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Discipline score**

Recommended baseline weighting:

-   Behavioural self-assessment: 60%

-   Situational judgment: 40%

Formula:

Discipline score = Behavioural score × 0.60 + Scenario score × 0.40

**Post-module score**

After completing a module:

-   Behavioural reassessment: 20%

-   Knowledge check: 15%

-   Situational judgment: 30%

-   Practice completion: 25%

-   Evidence reflection: 10%

**Development categories**

**Emerging: 0--49**

The learner may understand parts of the discipline but applies it
inconsistently.

**Developing: 50--69**

The learner demonstrates awareness and some effective behaviour but
remains vulnerable in difficult situations.

**Applied: 70--84**

The learner usually applies the discipline appropriately and can
recognise common failure modes.

**Integrated: 85--100**

The learner demonstrates consistent judgment, application and reflective
adjustment across contexts.

These labels should always be accompanied by explanation. A score must
never stand alone.

**Underuse and overuse**

High scores do not automatically mean balanced use.

Each module should include separate indicators for distortion.

For example, Detachment could be distorted through:

-   **Underuse:** controlling, defending, over-identifying

-   **Overuse:** withdrawing, indifference, refusal to take
    responsibility

The profile should therefore show:

-   current capacity;

-   likely underuse;

-   possible overuse;

-   and context sensitivity.

**15. Complete Module 1 --- Detachment**

**Module title**

**Detachment**

**Releasing attachment without abandoning responsibility**

**Central AI question**

**Am I evaluating this output, or defending it because I have become
invested in it?**

**Foundation**

Detachment includes neutrality, separation of responsibility for process
and content, the ability to support a group decision one may not
personally prefer, and freedom from the need for status, recognition or
credit. The facilitator must also maintain fairness toward people they
may personally like or dislike.

**AI-era definition**

**Detachment is the ability to evaluate an AI-generated output,
preferred solution or personal interpretation without allowing ego,
prior investment, status or the need to be right to control the
decision.**

Detachment does not mean indifference. It means remaining responsible
without becoming possessed by a preferred answer.

**Opening dilemma**

You lead a transformation team redesigning an internal approval process.
After several days of prompting and refinement, you present an
AI-generated workflow that is faster, cheaper and technically
sophisticated.

During the review, employees identify several situations the workflow
does not handle well. They propose a simpler alternative that retains
more human review.

You believe your design is more advanced. What should you do?

**Options**

A. Defend the AI-generated workflow because the employees may be
resisting change.

B. Withdraw your proposal and leave the group to decide without further
involvement.

C. Help the group compare both approaches against agreed criteria,
including operational performance, human impact and accountability.

D. Ask AI to produce stronger arguments supporting your preferred
workflow.

**Best response**

**C**

It preserves responsibility while releasing attachment to the preferred
solution.

**Why Detachment matters**

AI outputs can create attachment quickly.

The learner may become attached because:

-   they invested time in the prompt;

-   the output sounds impressive;

-   it supports an existing belief;

-   it strengthens their status;

-   they have already presented it publicly;

-   or abandoning it feels like admitting failure.

A person can become more committed to a generated answer than the
evidence justifies.

Detachment protects the ability to say:

-   This is useful, but incomplete.

-   This was my idea, but it is not the best option.

-   The group may need to own this decision.

-   The model's confidence is not evidence.

-   I can change direction without losing credibility.

**What Detachment is**

-   Releasing the need to be right

-   Separating evaluation from ego

-   Recognising AI as an input rather than an authority

-   Allowing people to influence decisions that affect them

-   Letting go of sunk costs

-   Remaining fair toward opposing perspectives

-   Supporting learning rather than imposing correction

**What Detachment is not**

-   Lack of care

-   Avoiding accountability

-   Refusing to recommend a course of action

-   Treating every option as equally valid

-   Allowing harmful decisions to proceed unchallenged

-   Disengaging when disagreement appears

-   Pretending not to have values

**Central tension**

Detachment is balanced by Engagement.

**Without Detachment**

I may:

-   control;

-   defend;

-   impose;

-   personalise disagreement;

-   or use AI to strengthen authority.

**Without Engagement**

I may:

-   withdraw;

-   refuse responsibility;

-   avoid difficult intervention;

-   or claim neutrality when action is required.

**Balanced expression**

I can care deeply about the quality of the outcome without needing the
outcome to validate me.

**AI-era failure modes**

**Automation bias**

Accepting AI output because it appears authoritative.

**Prompt ownership**

Treating the generated answer as personal intellectual property because
one created the prompt.

**Sunk-cost attachment**

Continuing with a weak solution because of time already invested.

**Fluency capture**

Confusing polished expression with sound reasoning.

**Status defence**

Resisting correction because the output has already been presented to
leaders.

**Artificial consensus**

Using numerous generated arguments to overwhelm dissent.

**Lesson content**

AI can produce a proposal so complete that it appears ready for
implementation. That completeness may trigger premature loyalty.

The effective facilitator asks:

-   What evidence supports this?

-   What evidence challenges it?

-   What is missing?

-   Who is affected?

-   Who should own the decision?

-   Am I protecting the solution, or serving the purpose?

-   Would I judge this differently if someone else had generated it?

Detachment becomes especially important when the "best" decision and the
"owned" decision are not the same.

A technically superior decision may fail if:

-   affected people do not understand it;

-   employees cannot implement it;

-   the process damages trust;

-   local knowledge was excluded;

-   or people have no meaningful route to challenge it.

The facilitator must decide whether the situation requires:

-   correction;

-   participation;

-   experimentation;

-   learning;

-   or release.

**16. Complete Module 2 --- Intentionality**

**Module title**

**Intentionality**

**Choosing what the technology is for**

**Central AI question**

**Why am I using AI here?**

**Foundation**

Intentionality connects with focused energy, humility, awareness of
weakness, empathy, preparation and alignment with the desired outcome.
It includes the reflective questions: "What am I doing and why?" and
"What is the group doing?"

**AI-era definition**

**Intentionality is the capacity to clarify the purpose, values and
human responsibility governing AI use before selecting a tool,
generating an output or automating a process.**

Intentionality prevents the tool from defining the task.

**Opening dilemma**

A government department plans to introduce AI into its public-enquiry
service. The stated objective is to improve access and reduce waiting
times.

During planning, the project gradually becomes focused on reducing
staffing costs. The team proposes automating most interactions,
including complex cases.

What should happen next?

**Options**

A. Continue, because cost reduction is a legitimate result of
innovation.

B. Pause and clarify whether the new design still serves the original
public-service purpose.

C. Ask AI to identify further opportunities to reduce human involvement.

D. Launch the system and review the purpose after implementation.

**Best response**

**B**

Intentionality requires examining whether the means remain aligned with
the intended outcome.

**Why Intentionality matters**

AI encourages action before purpose is clear.

A learner may begin with:

-   Which tool should we use?

-   What can we automate?

-   What can the model produce?

-   How quickly can we launch?

The discipline of Intentionality begins earlier:

-   What are we trying to achieve?

-   Why does it matter?

-   Who should benefit?

-   What values must be protected?

-   What must remain human?

-   What should not be done simply because it is possible?

Without Intentionality, convenience can silently become the objective.

**What Intentionality is**

-   Clarifying purpose

-   Naming motives

-   Aligning means and ends

-   Identifying values and responsibilities

-   Determining appropriate boundaries

-   Preparing deliberately

-   Recognising hidden agendas

-   Choosing AI rather than defaulting to it

**What Intentionality is not**

-   Controlling every outcome

-   Refusing flexibility

-   Assuming good intentions guarantee good impact

-   Using purpose statements as public relations

-   Treating technology as neutral

-   Demanding certainty before beginning

-   Believing intention excuses harmful consequences

**Central tension**

Intentionality must remain open to feedback.

**Underused**

It becomes:

-   reactive;

-   tool-led;

-   unclear;

-   convenience-driven;

-   easily distracted.

**Balanced**

It is:

-   purposeful;

-   ethically aware;

-   adaptive;

-   explicit about responsibility.

**Overused**

It becomes:

-   rigid;

-   controlling;

-   unable to revise;

-   attached to an idealised outcome;

-   dismissive of emergence.

  defining outcomes      first                  a fixed plan

**AI-era failure modes**

**Tool-first thinking**

Beginning with the technology rather than the human problem.

**Purpose drift**

A project begins with one goal and gradually serves another.

**Efficiency substitution**

Speed or cost becomes the measure of success even when the original aim
was access, fairness or quality.

**Responsibility laundering**

AI is used to create distance from a difficult decision.

**Avoidance automation**

A task is automated because a person does not want to perform the human
responsibility involved.

**Capability seduction**

The organisation does something because AI makes it possible, not
because it should be done.

**Lesson content**

Intentionality asks the learner to distinguish between the stated
purpose and the operative purpose.

For example:

**Stated purpose**

Improve access to public services.

**Possible operative purpose**

Reduce cost by reducing staff interaction.

These purposes may coexist, but they are not identical.

The effective facilitator names competing motives rather than hiding
them.

A responsible AI project should clarify:

-   intended outcome;

-   affected people;

-   public or organisational value;

-   unacceptable consequences;

-   human accountability;

-   non-delegable decisions;

-   indicators of success.

The most important question is not:

What can AI do?

It is:

What are we responsible for doing well?

**Recognition activity --- What is the real purpose?**

Match the action with the likely hidden motive.

**Action**

Possible motives:

-   Improve clarity

-   Avoid discomfort

-   Save time

-   Maintain professional tone

The learner must identify that several motives may be present.

The activity then asks:

Which motive creates the greatest risk if it remains unexamined?

Best answer:

Avoiding the responsibility of directly engaging with the employee.

**The Why Stack**

The learner answers "Why?" five times.

Example:

I want AI to prepare the decision memo.

Why?

To save time.

Why is saving time important?

Because the deadline is close.

Why is the deadline close?

Because I postponed making the decision.

Why did I postpone it?

Because the decision may upset several people.

What does that reveal?

AI is partly being used to accelerate work and partly to create distance
from responsibility.

**Self-assessment**

1.  I define the human outcome before selecting an AI tool.

2.  I can clearly explain why AI is appropriate for a task.

3.  I identify what should not be delegated.

4.  I examine whether convenience is replacing responsibility.

5.  I consider how the process reflects organisational or personal
    values.

6.  I name competing motives honestly.

7.  I usually start with what the tool can do rather than what the
    situation requires. **Reverse-scored**

8.  Once I establish an intention, I find it difficult to revise it.
    **Overuse indicator**

**17. Complete Module 3 --- Sense of Wonder**

**Module title**

**Sense of Wonder**

**Seeing what is not yet there**

**Defining statement**

**AI may expand what can be generated. Sense of Wonder preserves our
capacity to imagine.**

**Central AI question**

**Am I using AI to expand possibility, or allowing it to define the
limits of what I can imagine?**

**Foundation**

Sense of Wonder is openness to surprise before experience is placed into
a familiar category. It involves holding fear and fascination together,
allowing one's existing image of a group or situation to be challenged,
and developing respect for the depth and possibility present. It is
connected to wonder with creativity, motivation, trust and greater
awareness.

**AI-era definition**

**Sense of Wonder is the capacity to remain curious, receptive and
imaginatively alive in the presence of uncertainty, so that
possibilities can be perceived before they are reduced to familiar
categories, inherited assumptions or machine-generated patterns.**

**Opening dilemma**

A government agency is digitising a public-benefit application process.

AI is used to analyse the current forms, approval criteria, rejection
reasons and processing delays. It produces a redesigned workflow that is
faster, less expensive and mostly automated.

The proposal preserves the existing eligibility categories and
documentation requirements while moving them online.

Senior leaders are impressed and want implementation to begin
immediately.

What is the most important question the project team has not yet asked?

**Options**

A. Which AI platform will process applications fastest?

B. How can the existing process be automated with fewer staff?

C. What could the service become if it were redesigned around people's
needs rather than the current procedure?

D. How quickly can users be trained to complete the new digital form?

**Best response**

**C**

Sense of Wonder asks the team to imagine transformation rather than
merely automate the past.

**Why Sense of Wonder matters**

AI can provide an answer before the question is fully experienced.

A polished output may create premature completion.

Once the complete proposal is seen, you may begin:

-   editing the AI's idea;

-   improving its framing;

-   selecting among its options;

-   or defending its assumptions.

The imaginative field has already narrowed.

The risk is not simply that AI will produce creative work. The deeper
risk is that people may stop practising the conditions from which
creativity grows:

-   attention;

-   curiosity;

-   ambiguity;

-   incubation;

-   independent perception;

-   play;

-   surprise;

-   and the courage to explore what is not yet validated.

**Wonder and creativity**

Creativity does not begin with production.

It begins with:

1.  Openness

2.  Attention

3.  Curiosity

4.  Imagination

5.  Courage

6.  Expression

AI is highly capable at expression and variation.

Human creativity, however, also requires:

-   sensing what is missing;

-   asking a question no one has framed;

-   recognising meaning;

-   connecting lived experience;

-   imagining a future not contained in past data;

-   and deciding what deserves to be created.

**What Sense of Wonder is**

-   Remaining open before concluding

-   Seeing possibility beyond precedent

-   Allowing surprise

-   Protecting incubation

-   Questioning inherited categories

-   Imagining what does not yet exist

-   Recognising human potential beyond prediction

-   Exploring before optimising

**What Sense of Wonder is not**

-   Accepting every new idea

-   Rejecting evidence

-   Romanticising novelty

-   Avoiding practical constraints

-   Remaining permanently undecided

-   Treating surprise as proof

-   Generating endlessly without choosing

-   Assuming imagination removes responsibility

**Central tension**

Wonder must be balanced with discernment and action.

**Underused**

It becomes:

-   closed;

-   overly certain;

-   dependent on precedent;

-   anchored by first answers;

-   dismissive of incomplete ideas.

**Balanced**

It is:

-   open;

-   curious;

-   discerning;

-   willing to incubate;

-   capable of choosing and developing.

**Overused**

It becomes:

-   endlessly exploratory;

-   novelty-seeking;

-   unwilling to commit;

-   detached from feasibility;

-   inspired but inactive.

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  **Underused**             **Balanced**                **Overused**

  Accepts first plausible   Explores several frames     Generates
  answer                                                endlessly

  Automates existing        Reimagines the underlying   Rejects useful
  assumptions               purpose                     structure

  Treats prediction as      Sees patterns and           Ignores evidence

**AI-era failure modes**

**Premature completion**

Receiving a polished answer before the question has matured.

**Anchoring**

Allowing the first generated framing to determine all later thought.

**Historical capture**

Assuming the future must resemble available data.

**Template dependency**

Beginning with examples rather than observation.

**Creative displacement**

Allowing AI to produce the initial idea, direction and meaning.

**Prediction as identity**

Treating a person or group as equivalent to a model's category or
forecast.

**Productivity confusion**

Assuming more outputs mean more creativity.

**Lesson content**

AI systems generate from patterns. They can identify likely combinations
from what already exists.

Human imagination can commit to what is not yet likely.

This matters when organisations transform services.

Without Sense of Wonder, an organisation may:

-   digitise the form;

-   automate the rejection;

-   accelerate the transaction;

-   reproduce historical categories;

-   and measure success through speed and cost.

With Sense of Wonder, it may ask:

-   Why does this form exist?

-   What need is the person actually trying to meet?

-   What categories exclude people?

-   What would access look like if the current process did not exist?

-   Where does human judgment create value?

-   What possibility is absent from the historical records?

-   What should be created rather than automated?

Wonder does not reject current reality.

It holds two truths:

We must see reality accurately.\
Reality is not finished.
